<p class="lead">Three roles: holder, <Term id="spender">spender</Term>, recipient. Approving and spending are two separate actions.</p>

## Why do wallets keep asking you to "approve" rather than "pay"?

Many <Term id="dex-amm">decentralised exchanges</Term> and services use two steps. First, the holder **approves** a <Term id="smart-contract">smart contract</Term> to use up to a set amount of a token. This is `approve`. Later, when you act, that contract calls `transferFrom`.

- **`approve` is not a payment**: it only writes into the token contract "B may move up to this much of A's tokens". **Balances do not change at all.**
- What actually moves balances is a later `transferFrom` (move) or `burnFrom` (<Term id="burn">destroy</Term>) sent by the spender.
- An <Term id="approve">approval</Term> **stays valid** until the <Term id="allowance">allowance</Term> is used up or the holder sends another `approve` to change it.

So although an approval is not "money already gone", a long-lived approval is a standing permission for future deductions.

“Balances do not change” refers to this token’s balances. Sending an approval transaction still costs a network fee. Looking up the allowance is read-only and needs no signature or fee.

## An allowance is an overwritable "remaining amount"

- After part of it is used, the rest **stays valid**: in the lab B burned 100 and 400 remained.
- A new `approve` **overwrites the allowance. It does not add to it.** If the allowance is 100 and you `approve(B, 500)`, the result is 500, not 600.
- To clear it, send `approve(B, 0)`.

## Three risks and one habit

1. **Too large an allowance**: if the approved contract has a bug or malicious logic, a bigger allowance means a bigger possible loss.
2. **Approving the wrong address**: a familiar name does not make an address trustworthy (remember Chapter 9: names are not unique).
3. **Forgotten approvals**: after you stop using a service, the allowance may still be usable.

**One habit**: at the end of every experiment, check `allowance`. When you no longer need it, send `approve(spender, 0)` to revoke it.

<Callout type="warn" title="Revoking has a time window too">

A revocation <Term id="transaction">transaction</Term> must be sent by the **holder**, and takes effect only once it is mined. Until then, the old allowance can still be used.

</Callout>

## Everyday analogy

You let a food-delivery platform deduct up to 100 loyalty points in total. Nothing is deducted yet. Each use reduces the remaining allowance. **It does not reset every month.** When you stop using the service, set the limit to 0. That withdraws permission. It does not hand anyone 100 points.

The lab initially left an allowance of 100. D-01 later revoked it. The next chapter links that successful transaction. Look for an `Approval` event with value 0. Confirm that no token transfer occurred. The early leftover amount is not the snapshot state.
