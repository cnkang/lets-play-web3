## Start by looking, or read in order

You do not need to connect a wallet, claim test coins or create an account to read. To see a real action first, try the [read-only exercise in Chapter 8](../08-lab-counter/#inspect-a-transaction): open an existing transaction and check its status, token transfers and event. Then return to the reading path below. Learning to inspect a record is a useful first hands-on step.

## Four kinds of content

1. **Principles and everyday analogies** explain <Term id="blockchain">blockchains</Term> and <Term id="smart-contract">smart contracts</Term>, using shared records and vending machines as examples. An analogy helps explain an idea; it is neither a definition nor an experimental result.
2. **On-chain results** come from deployments, <Term id="transaction">transactions</Term> and upgrades on <Term id="ethereum">Ethereum</Term>’s Sepolia testnet on 11–12 November 2026 (Singapore time). Look up addresses, <Term id="tx-hash">transaction hashes</Term> (the IDs used to find transactions) and <Term id="block-number">block numbers</Term> on Sepolia <Term id="etherscan">Etherscan</Term>. Read balances at the recorded block; today’s values do not replace the historical snapshot.
3. **Local tests and simulations** supply test counts, assertions and the UBOX V4 simulation. These are local records, not explorer transactions. V4 was never deployed on-chain. Chapter 15 explains the review’s results and limits.
4. **Future plans** describe work still to do. Phase-0 basics, experiments and review are complete. Phase-1 means the next DeFi work, which has not started. A plan is not a completed experiment.

Each chapter can be read on its own, but reading in order works best because later experiments use earlier ideas. The experiments are not tucked away in a separate "lab log": each one lives in the chapter it illustrates. The Counter contract follows the chapter on smart contracts, and the allowance revocation follows "Approval is not payment".

## Rules for the Phase-0 labs

The whole lab followed a few rules. The rules are themselves habits worth learning:

- **The budget was strictly $0.** No crypto was bought, no mainnet funds were used, and no real wallet seed phrase or private key was imported.
- **Only the Ethereum Sepolia testnet** was used, together with free, open-source, widely used tools (solc, <Term id="viem">viem</Term>, the <Term id="openzeppelin">OpenZeppelin</Term> contracts library, <Term id="foundry">Foundry</Term>).
- **Every on-chain transaction was <Term id="simulate">simulated</Term> first (a free rehearsal) and <Term id="broadcast">broadcast</Term> (actually sent to the network) only after explicit confirmation.** Signing in, <Term id="signature">signing</Term>, approving and broadcasting all required confirmation first; if safety could not be confirmed, work stopped.
- Every experiment recorded the network, what was done, the transaction hash or contract address, the actual cost, what was learned and the next step.
- Test-account private keys lived only in a local, Git-ignored, permission-restricted folder. They were never printed or written into any public record. This book contains no private keys, seed phrases or <Term id="api-key">API keys</Term>.

## After reading, you should be able to answer

- Why do a group of merchants sometimes need a shared ledger, and sometimes not at all?
- Looking at one transaction in a <Term id="block-explorer">block explorer</Term>, can you tell "the transaction succeeded" from "a balance changed" from "only an approval was granted"?
- How do holding <Term id="token">tokens</Term>, approving tokens and upgrading a contract differ as kinds of power?
- Why can neither a transfer to the wrong address nor any confirmed transaction be undone?
- Why can a review still have findings when all 49 <Term id="unit-test">unit tests</Term> pass?
- Why does "passed in a local simulation" not mean "live on-chain"?
