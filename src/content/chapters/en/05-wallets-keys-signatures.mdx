<p class="lead">A <Term id="wallet">wallet</Term> mainly holds the power to sign, not a drawer inside some app where tokens sit.</p>

## Your tokens are not actually "in" the wallet

When you pay by bank card, the bank checks your identity and account permissions. On networks like <Term id="ethereum">Ethereum</Term>, an ordinary account uses a **<Term id="private-key">private key</Term>** to produce a **<Term id="signature">digital signature</Term>**, and the network checks the signature to confirm the <Term id="transaction">transaction</Term> was authorised.

- An **address** is like a public account number. It is safe to share.
- A **private key** is like a master permission that can never be reset: whoever holds it can sign anything in that address's name.
- Your token balance is actually recorded **in a contract on-chain** (for example, a balance table inside an <Term id="erc20">ERC-20</Term> contract). The wallet just reads it for you and signs on your behalf when you agree.

<Figure name="wallet-flow" n="2" alt="Five steps: the wallet signs with a local private key; the signed transaction; an RPC node checks it and holds it in the mempool; a validator includes it in a block; the explorer shows network, status, from, to, logs, block and hash.">
A signed transaction is broadcast, checked by nodes, included in a block by a validator, and then observed in a block explorer. The private key never leaves the wallet.
</Figure>

## Four actions, very different risks

- **Connecting a wallet** tells a website one of your addresses. On its own it does not move any tokens.
- **Signing a message** may be a harmless log-in statement, or it may grant a risky permission. **Read it before you sign.**
- **Sending a transaction** changes on-chain state and usually costs a <Term id="gas">gas</Term> fee.
- **Exporting a <Term id="seed-phrase">seed phrase</Term> or private key** means anyone who obtains it may control everything at that address. Legitimate sites almost never need you to do this.

## Seed phrases, private keys and API keys

All of these may have "key" in their name, but the risks differ completely:

<T label="Private key, seed phrase, API key and address compared">

| Name | What it can do | If it leaks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Private key | Sign transactions: transfer, deploy contracts, approve tokens | Someone can spend everything at that address |
| Seed phrase (usually 12 or 24 words) | Restore a whole wallet (possibly many keys) | Worse than one key: the entire wallet is taken over |
| API key (for example Etherscan's) | Call a website's API in your name | Usually cannot move assets, but your quota can be used up or abused in your name |
| Address | A public "house number" | Not a secret; being misled to a fake address is the real risk |

</T>

## How the labs handled this

- Accounts A and B were both **freshly generated locally** as disposable test accounts. No real wallet seed phrase was ever imported, and neither was ever used on <Term id="mainnet">mainnet</Term>.
- The private keys lived only in a local, Git-ignored folder (folder permission 700, file permission 600). They were never printed and never written into public records or this book.
- Broadcast scripts checked that the account's ETH balance was **no more than 0.1 ETH**, as a guard against accidentally using a key that might hold mainnet funds.

For experiments, use a wallet that is **completely separate** from your everyday funds, and work only on the Sepolia <Term id="testnet">testnet</Term>. **Never** import a real wallet's seed phrase or private key into any lab, website, chat or code repository.
