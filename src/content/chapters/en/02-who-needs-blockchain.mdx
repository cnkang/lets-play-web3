<p class="lead">If several shops share loyalty points, who should keep the records? A <Term id="blockchain">blockchain</Term> lets them share a record they can each check. Whether they need one depends on who they already trust.</p>

## A community loyalty-points story

Imagine a few local shops issuing loyalty points together: you earn points at the café, redeem them at the corner shop, and the gym accepts them too.

- If everyone trusts one platform to keep the books, an **ordinary database** is enough: the platform records an entry and everyone follows it.
- If no shop is willing to hand final control of the <Term id="shared-ledger">ledger</Term> to another shop, because it fears quiet edits or later denial, then a blockchain is worth discussing: every shop can independently check the same history, and no single shop can easily rewrite it.

## Three questions to ask

Before choosing a blockchain, ask:

1. Are there **several independent parties** who must keep one consistent shared state?
2. Do you need it to be **publicly verifiable**, to reduce one-sided edits and endless reconciliation?
3. Are those benefits **worth the costs** in fees, performance, privacy and operational complexity?

If any answer is "no", an ordinary system is usually the better choice.

## A comparison table

<T label="Everyday scenarios: ordinary system vs on-chain">

| Scenario | When an ordinary system fits better | What on-chain might add |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A bank's internal ledger | One bank manages everything centrally | Usually no blockchain needed |
| Points honoured across organisations | A platform already trusted by everyone | Many parties verify the same transaction history |
| Self-executing digital vouchers | One company runs everything | Public rules that anyone can check |
| Product provenance and anti-counterfeiting | The data captured at the source is hard to guarantee | Records are hard for one party to rewrite, but **the source is not guaranteed to be true** |

</T>

<Callout type="note" title="Key idea">

Learn what a blockchain can do, and when it is not worth using. If everyone already trusts the same record-keeper, a central database is simpler, faster, cheaper and better for privacy.

</Callout>

## How this connects to the labs

This book uses four environments. Keep them apart:

1. **Browser interactive simulations** (the lab pages on this site): nothing goes on-chain, and there is no public hash to look up.
2. **Historical public Sepolia transactions**: look them up on a <Term id="sepolia">Sepolia</Term> <Term id="block-explorer">block explorer</Term>.
3. **Local Anvil forks** (Chapters 17–18): a read-only copy of historical Sepolia state, then writes on that local copy. Local hashes are not look-up-able on a public explorer.
4. **A fully local Anvil teaching chain** (Chapter 19): no Sepolia fork; those hashes are not look-up-able on a public explorer either.

A <Term id="testnet">testnet</Term>'s "money" is free and has no market value. The publicly verifiable parts exist so you can see jointly checkable bookkeeping: anyone can look up those public <Term id="transaction">transactions</Term>. Real on-chain changes still cost a fee (<Term id="gas">gas</Term>), which Chapter 6 explains.
