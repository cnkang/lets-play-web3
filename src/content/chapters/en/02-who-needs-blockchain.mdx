<p class="lead">Start by treating a blockchain as a way to keep shared books when the participants do not fully trust one another, not as magic new technology.</p>

## A community loyalty-points story

Imagine a few local shops issuing loyalty points together: you earn points at the café, redeem them at the corner shop, and the gym accepts them too.

- If everyone trusts one platform to keep the books, an **ordinary database** is enough: the platform records an entry and everyone follows it.
- If no shop is willing to hand final control of the ledger to another shop, because it fears quiet edits or later denial, then a blockchain is worth discussing: every shop can independently check the same history, and no single shop can easily rewrite it.

## Three questions to ask

Before choosing a blockchain, ask:

1. Are there **several independent parties** who must keep one consistent shared state?
2. Do you need it to be **publicly verifiable**, to reduce one-sided edits and endless reconciliation?
3. Are those benefits **worth the costs** in fees, performance, privacy and operational complexity?

If any answer is "no", an ordinary system is usually the better choice.

## A comparison table

<T label="Everyday scenarios: ordinary system vs on-chain">

| Scenario | When an ordinary system fits better | What on-chain might add |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A bank's internal ledger | One bank manages everything centrally | Usually no blockchain needed |
| Points honoured across organisations | A platform already trusted by everyone | Many parties verify the same transaction history |
| Self-executing digital vouchers | One company runs everything | Public rules that anyone can check |
| Product provenance and anti-counterfeiting | The data captured at the source is hard to guarantee | Records are hard for one party to rewrite, but **the source is not guaranteed to be true** |

</T>

<Callout type="note" title="Key idea">

The goal is not only to know what a blockchain can do, but also when it is not worth it. If everyone already trusts the same record-keeper, a central database is simpler, faster, cheaper and better for privacy.

</Callout>

## How this connects to the labs

All the later experiments run on the Sepolia **testnet**. Its "money" has no market value; the point is to see with your own eyes what jointly verifiable bookkeeping looks like: anyone can look up every transaction in a block explorer. That is the "publicly verifiable" of this chapter. The price is that every change costs a fee (gas), which Chapter 6 explains.
