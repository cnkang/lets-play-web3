## Start with an everyday picture

Picture an **automatic currency exchange machine** at an airport. It holds two currencies, say dollars and euros. You put in dollars, and a formula decides how many euros come out. There is no clerk, and no other customer has to match your order.

- The more money the machine holds, the less one exchange moves its rate. If it holds little, one big exchange pushes the rate far off.
- Each exchange pays a small fee. The fees go to **the people who put money into the machine**.
- The machine only follows its formula. It never checks the rate outside. If someone set the rate wrong at the start, it keeps trading at that rate until someone "takes the bargain" and pushes it back.

This machine is a <Term id="liquidity-pool">liquidity pool</Term> on a <Term id="dex-amm">DEX / AMM</Term>. Every lab below works with machines like this.

## Basic ideas

### Swaps, pools and fee tiers

- **<Term id="swap">Swap</Term>**: exchange one token for another. On a DEX the other side is a pool contract, not a person.
- **Pools and <Term id="fee-tier">fee tiers</Term>**: one token pair can have several pools with different fees. Uniswap v3 commonly uses 0.01%, 0.05%, 0.3% and 1%. Pools can show very different prices. In this chapter two pools differed by almost ten thousand times.
- Tokens used here: the <Term id="stablecoin">stablecoins</Term> USDC (<Addr a="0x1c7D4B196Cb0C7B01d743Fbc6116a902379C7238" short />) and EURC (<Addr a="0x08210F9170F89Ab7658F0B5E3fF39b0E03C594D4" short />), plus <Term id="weth">WETH</Term> (<Addr a="0xfFf9976782d46CC05630D1f6eBAb18b2324d6B14" short />). The addresses come from the official Circle and Uniswap docs and were checked on-chain.

### v2 and v3: two kinds of machine

<T label="Differences between Uniswap v2 and v3">

| Item | Uniswap v2 | Uniswap v3 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pricing | Constant product `x·y=k`: the two token amounts multiplied stay constant | <Term id="concentrated-liquidity">Concentrated liquidity</Term>: each provider puts money only inside a price range they choose |
| Price from balances? | Yes: price ≈ the ratio of the two balances | **No**: total balances do not show how much sits near the current price |
| Receipt | An <Term id="lp-token">LP token</Term> (the same for everyone) | An <Term id="nft">NFT</Term> (every position has its own range) |

</T>

A real counter-example: the WETH/USDC 0.05% pool address held 121.46 WETH and 53,413,101 USDC. Applying `x·y=k` gives about 439,770 USDC/WETH, but the pool's real price was about 30,000. That is more than ten times off. **To read a v3 price, read the pool's current price or ask the quoter contract. Do not divide the balances.**

### Price impact, slippage, minimum received and deadline

- **<Term id="price-impact">Price impact</Term>**: how much your own order moves the price. The odd pool in this chapter held only about 1,630 USDC. Selling a single EURC moved its price from about 152.75 to about 123.9.
- **<Term id="slippage">Slippage</Term>**: the difference between the amount quoted for your swap and the amount you receive. The quote already accounts for the size of your order. If someone trades before you, the pool can give you a different amount.
- **<Term id="min-received">Minimum received</Term>**: the floor you set. If the swap would pay less when it runs, the whole transaction reverts and you lose only the fee. It is a floor, not a forecast, and not a limit order. Setting it to 0 removes the protection.
- **<Term id="deadline">Deadline</Term>**: after this time the transaction is void. Every swap here used a deadline of about 20 minutes.

## Approvals: give exactly what is needed

To swap an ERC-20 token such as USDC, you first <Term id="approve">approve</Term> the router to pull a set amount from you. An approval is only a standing permission. The amount still available is the <Term id="allowance">allowance</Term>.

- **Exact approval**: every approval here covered exactly the amount being spent. For 1 USDC the approval was `1000000`, because USDC has 6 decimals. After the swap, the allowance was back to 0.
- **<Term id="infinite-approval">Infinite approval</Term>**: many sites ask you to approve the maximum so you never approve again. If that contract is buggy or fake, it can drain all of that token from your wallet. **This chapter never used an infinite approval.**
- **<Term id="permit2">Permit / Permit2 signatures</Term>**: these grant an allowance with a signature instead of a transaction. Phishing sites love them because they look free. **This chapter never signed any Permit or Permit2.** If your wallet asks you to sign a message with a token name, an amount and an expiry, treat it like an approval.
- **Revoke**: set allowances you no longer need back to 0 with `approve(spender, 0)`. After every round, the labs checked USDC, EURC and WETH allowances to every router and to Permit2. All were 0.

<Callout type="danger" title="⚠️ Only use contracts with verified source code">

The chain only guarantees that **bytecode** is public. Only <Term id="verification">source verification</Term> ties readable source to that bytecode. Open the contract on <Term id="etherscan">Etherscan</Term> and look for the **green check** next to the Contract tab ("Source Code Verified").

- **Treat any contract without verified source as a black box. Do not approve it and do not send it funds.**
- Check addresses against the project's official docs. Here, USDC and EURC came from Circle's docs and Uniswap addresses from docs.uniswap.org. Their code and settings were also checked on-chain.
- "Can be audited" is not "has been audited". An upgradeable contract can still change later.

</Callout>

### Why do odd pools exist?

- **Confirmed fact**: whoever creates a v3 pool sets its starting price. After that, the price depends only on the pool itself. **It never follows the outside market by itself.** It only moves when someone trades.
- **Guess (cannot be confirmed)**: a tester probably set a wild starting price and added liquidity in a wild range. Test tokens are worthless, so nobody bothers to correct it. We do not know who created the pool or why.

## Lab 3: arbitrage

The odd pool "pays a lot for EURC". So buy EURC cheaply in other pools and sell it into the odd pool. That is <Term id="arbitrage">arbitrage</Term>.

**How the best plan was found: marginal prices.** The more EURC you sell into the odd pool, the less each extra one earns. Buying works the other way: each extra EURC costs more.

<T label="Marginal price when selling into the odd pool (fork measurement)">

| EURC sold so far | 1 | 19 | 50 | 80 |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| USDC for this one EURC | ≈ 101.6 | ≈ 14.8 | ≈ 3.5 | ≈ 1.55 |

</T>

The script spent 0.5 USDC at a time on whichever pool bought the most EURC. It then checked how much more USDC those EURC would earn. It kept going while the extra earned beat the extra spent. It stopped where the marginal buy price (2.972) met the marginal sell price (2.970). The whole plan was rehearsed on a fork first:

<T label="Planned gain of three approaches">

| Approach | Planned USDC gain |
| --- | ---: |
| Sell only the 19 EURC already held | 815.04 |
| Buy and sell, without the v2 pool | 874.93 |
| Buy and sell, including the v2 pool (used) | 969.08 |

</T>

<Callout type="warn" title="On mainnet: do not expect free money">

- Mainnet has many arbitrage bots (<Term id="mev">MEV</Term> searchers). They usually close a gap within seconds, often in the same block. A person trading by hand will almost never win.
- Your transaction is public before it is included. Bots can trade just before you or "sandwich" you so you get a worse price. The minimum received is your floor against that.
- The testnet "profit" here was really the loss of the odd pool's liquidity provider. On mainnet, a provider who sets the wrong price loses real money in moments.
- Mainnet also has fake tokens, look-alike names and tokens you can buy but never sell. **When a price looks too good, first ask: "Is this a trap?"**

</Callout>

## Impermanent loss on paper

A <Term id="liquidity-provider">liquidity provider (LP)</Term> deposits two tokens and earns fees from other people's swaps. The cost is <Term id="impermanent-loss">impermanent loss</Term>: after the price moves, tokens in a pool are worth less than the same tokens simply held (HODL).

Paper set-up: starting price 1.10 USDC/EURC, deposit 10 USDC + 9.090909 EURC (worth 20 USDC), fee 0.3%, gas ignored.

<T label="v2 full-range impermanent loss (paper model)">

| Price becomes | LP value | HODL value | Impermanent loss | Fees needed to break even |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 0.2× | 8.944272 | 12 | −25.464401% | 3.055728 |
| 0.5× | 14.142136 | 15 | −5.719096% | 0.857864 |
| 0.8× | 17.888544 | 18 | −0.619201% | 0.111456 |
| 1.25× | 22.360680 | 22.5 | −0.619201% | 0.139320 |
| 2× | 28.284271 | 30 | −5.719096% | 1.715729 |
| 5× | 44.721360 | 60 | −25.464401% | 15.278640 |

</T>

- If the price doubles or halves, the loss is about 5.7%. At 5× or one fifth, it is about 25.5%. The loss depends only on how many times the price moved, not on the direction.
- At 2×, the provider needs 1.715729 USDC of fees to break even. If they own the whole pool, the pool must trade 571.91 USDC. If they own 1%, it must trade 57,190.96 USDC.
- In v3, put the same money only in the range from the starting price divided by 1.2 to the starting price multiplied by 1.2. Inside that range the liquidity is **11.477226 times** the full-range amount. Fees depend on how much people swap while the price is in range and how much liquidity other providers supply. But at 2× the impermanent loss grows to **−30.151829%**. Once the price leaves the range, the position turns entirely into one token and earns no fees.

<Callout type="danger" title="⚠️ Mainnet safety: anyone can take change left in a router">

**If you overpay in ETH, call `refundETH` in the same transaction.** ETH or tokens left in a router or the NFPM are like cash dropped on the street.

- **Why anyone can take it**: these contracts keep no books. They do not remember who overpaid. `refundETH()` sends **all** ETH the contract holds (`address(this).balance`) to **whoever calls it**. `sweepToken` does the same for tokens.
- **On mainnet**: MEV bots watch these contracts. A leftover balance is usually swept in the same block or the next one.
- **Rules**:
  1. When you pay in ETH, put `refundETH` in **the same multicall** as the mint or swap.
  2. To take WETH or tokens out of the contract, add `unwrapWETH9` or `sweepToken` with a minimum amount in the same multicall. Or set yourself as the recipient directly.
  3. **Never** send ETH or tokens straight to a router or the NFPM. Never pay in one transaction and refund in a later one.
  4. Check the wallet preview before signing. A call paid in ETH should show `refundETH`. If it does not, do not sign.

</Callout>

**The extra 1 wei.** In Lab 6, transaction 8 refunded A **1 wei** more than A had overpaid (1 ETH = 10¹⁸ wei). The investigation found the cause. About six and a half hours earlier, another address sent 1 ETH with transaction <Tx h="0x1295b1c29efc21ebbca1f3a4bee905be2e03bb4650c7ec89f0a40cdb2e9f471d" short /> to mint a position. The position needed only 0.999999999999999999 ETH, so 1 wei stayed in the NFPM. That sender did not call `refundETH` in the same transaction. Our multicall did, so their 1 wei was refunded to A as well. The 1 wei is worthless, but the mechanism is real. **With a large amount on mainnet, the swept money would be real money.**

## Surprises and easter eggs

These unexpected moments taught the most:

- **The first send attempt failed, and nothing was sent.** It stopped at the fork pre-check with a network error (`fetch failed`). The nonce did not change, which proves no transaction reached the chain. **Pre-check first and stop on failure.** That rule protected the account.
- **A run was interrupted, but nothing was duplicated.** One send script ended right after printing its last "broadcast" line. The chain shows four transactions with nonces 21, 22, 23 and 24, and no duplicates. **After an interruption, check the nonce and the chain before you re-run anything.**
- **SwapRouter02 on Sepolia does not support v2.** Its v2 factory address is the zero address. v2 had to go through V2Router02, which cost one more approval and one more transaction. **The same contract name can be configured differently on another network. Read the on-chain settings first.**
- **cirBTC has no pools at all.** Being able to get a token does not mean you can sell it. Before buying any token, check for a pool deep enough to sell into.
- **A self-swap earns back only about 2.3% of its own fee**, and **EURC dipped to about 17.37**. See Lab 5.
- **ETH goes into v3 and WETH comes out**, and **the fees were 0**. See Lab 6.
- **The rehearsal and the real run produced the same NFT number, #234636.** The v2 LP amount and withdrawals matched exactly too, because nobody else traded those pools during those minutes. **An exact match is luck, not the norm.**
- **One approval cost 137,757 gas.** On Sepolia, approvals of USDC and EURC each used 137,757 gas, far more than the tens of thousands that tutorials often quote. The reason was not investigated. **Use the current estimate and a fork measurement, not numbers from a tutorial.**
- **We took someone else's leftover 1 wei.** See the safety warning above.

## Safety checklist

1. Use only the testnet and test tokens. Test tokens have no value.
2. Check contract addresses against official docs. Only use contracts with verified source (the Etherscan green check).
3. Approve exactly the amount needed. No infinite approvals. Do not sign Permit or Permit2.
4. Give every swap a minimum received (never 0) and a deadline.
5. Simulate first. Then read the confirmation card: amounts, minimums, gas, expected fee and fee cap. Only then sign.
6. When paying in ETH, put `refundETH` in the same multicall. Never send money straight to a router.
7. Afterwards, check that every allowance is 0 and the router holds none of your funds.
8. After an error or interruption, check the nonce and the chain before you send again.
9. When a price looks too good, suspect a trap first.

## Summary

- On a DEX your counterparty is a pool contract. v2 prices with `x·y=k`. v3 lets providers concentrate money in a price range.
- Your quote accounts for the price impact of your order. Compare the quote with what you received to measure slippage. The minimum received is your floor, and the deadline stops a stale transaction.
- Approve exact amounts and reset them to 0. Avoid infinite approvals and unknown Permit signatures. Only use contracts with verified source.
- Odd testnet pools can be arbitraged. On mainnet, bots take such chances within seconds.
- Providing liquidity earns fees and carries impermanent loss. v3 magnifies both and earns nothing out of range.
- Refund overpaid ETH in the same transaction. Anyone can take change left in a router.
